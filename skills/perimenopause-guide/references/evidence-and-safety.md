# Evidence and safety map

Last reviewed: 2026-09-02.

This reference keeps common answers aligned with current guidance. Recheck the linked source before giving personalized medical guidance, treatment comparisons, medication risks, supplement advice, or diagnostic criteria.

## Core sources

- [NICE NG23: Menopause — identification and management](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23/) — current UK guideline; last updated 15 April 2026.
- [NICE NG23 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23/chapter/Recommendations) — identification, testing, HRT, CBT, genitourinary symptoms, early menopause, and treatment review.
- [ACOG: Hormone Therapy for Menopause](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/hormone-therapy-for-menopause) — patient-facing benefits, forms, risks, and major contraindications.
- [ACOG: Perimenopausal Bleeding and Bleeding After Menopause](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/perimenopausal-bleeding-and-bleeding-after-menopause) — abnormal bleeding patterns and evaluation.
- [NHS: Postmenopausal bleeding](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/post-menopausal-bleeding/) — any bleeding after menopause needs assessment, including a single episode of spotting.
- [The Menopause Society: Position Statements](https://menopause.org/professional-resources/position-statements) — specialist consensus statements, including hormone therapy.

Use the person’s country when choosing additional sources because drug approvals, referral pathways, screening, and treatment availability vary.

## Stable orientation points

These are useful starting points, not a diagnosis:

- Perimenopause is the transition leading up to menopause. Menopause is generally recognized after 12 months without a menstrual period when another cause or hormonal method does not explain the absence.
- Common patterns include cycle change, hot flushes or night sweats, sleep disruption, genitourinary symptoms, mood changes, sexual difficulties, and musculoskeletal symptoms. None is unique to perimenopause.
- NICE recommends identifying perimenopause without laboratory tests in otherwise healthy people aged 45 or over who have new vasomotor symptoms and menstrual-cycle changes. Testing decisions differ for younger people, people using hormones, or atypical presentations.
- Hormone therapy is an effective option for vasomotor symptoms, but route, formulation, benefits, and risks are individualized. Someone with a uterus generally needs endometrial protection when systemic estrogen is used. Confirm regional guidance and personal contraindications before going further.
- Menopause-specific CBT is an evidence-based option for vasomotor symptoms and can be used with HRT, when HRT is unsuitable, or when someone prefers not to use it.
- Supplements and “biohacking” claims require extra skepticism. Check product quality, interactions, contraindications, and the evidence for the exact symptom and formulation. Do not extrapolate from a nutrient association to a treatment claim.

## Escalation

Lead with escalation when it applies.

### Emergency now

- chest pain, severe trouble breathing, fainting, or stroke signs;
- suicidal intent or inability to stay safe; or
- bleeding that is uncontrolled or accompanied by severe weakness, dizziness, or collapse.

Advise local emergency services or the nearest emergency department. Do not continue with routine coaching first.

### Prompt clinical assessment

- any vaginal bleeding after 12 months without a period, even once or as spotting;
- bleeding between periods, after sex, much heavier than usual, or lasting longer than usual;
- a new breast lump, persistent pelvic pain, unexplained weight loss, or severe symptoms;
- suspected menopause before age 45, especially before 40; or
- symptoms that are persistent, worsening, or substantially affecting daily life.

Do not imply that these signs are cancer or another specific condition. Explain that they deserve evaluation because several causes are possible and earlier assessment matters.

## High-risk answer checks

Before answering about HRT, non-hormonal prescription drugs, vaginal estrogen, contraception, supplements, or abnormal bleeding:

1. Verify current guidance.
2. Account for age, uterus status when relevant, current medications, pregnancy possibility, and key history the person volunteered.
3. Avoid personal safety conclusions from missing history.
4. Distinguish education from a recommendation to start, stop, or change treatment.
5. State the next appropriate clinician conversation in specific, non-alarmist language.
